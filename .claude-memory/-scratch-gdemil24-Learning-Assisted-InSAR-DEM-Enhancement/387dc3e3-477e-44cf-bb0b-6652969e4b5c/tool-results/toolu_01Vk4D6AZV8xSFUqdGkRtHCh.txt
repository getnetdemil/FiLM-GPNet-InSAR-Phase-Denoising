    38→## 1. Background: What is InSAR?
    39→
    40→### 1.1 Synthetic Aperture Radar Basics
    41→
    42→Synthetic Aperture Radar (SAR) is an active microwave imaging system mounted on a
    43→satellite. Unlike optical sensors, SAR emits its own electromagnetic pulses and records
    44→the returned echoes — which means it operates day and night and through clouds.
    45→
    46→Each recorded SAR pixel is a **complex number** (Single Look Complex, SLC). It has:
    47→- **Amplitude** (|SLC|): backscatter intensity — roughly how strongly the surface reflects
    48→  microwave energy. Depends on surface roughness, moisture, and dielectric properties.
    49→- **Phase** (∠SLC): the fractional round-trip travel time of the radar pulse to the surface
    50→  pixel, modulo 2π. Encodes range distance to sub-wavelength precision.
    51→
    52→**Capella Space specifications used in this project:**
    53→- X-band carrier: 9.6 GHz → wavelength λ ≈ 3.1 cm
    54→- Polarisation: HH (horizontal transmit, horizontal receive)
    55→- Mode: Spotlight (steered antenna → finer azimuth resolution)
    56→- Pixel spacing: ~0.35–0.5 m in both range and azimuth directions
    57→- Format: CInt16 complex integer GeoTIFF + extended JSON sidecar
    58→
    59→At λ=3.1 cm, one full 2π phase cycle corresponds to λ/2 = 1.55 cm of range displacement.
    60→This extraordinary sensitivity is what makes X-band InSAR powerful — and what makes noise
    61→such a critical problem.
    62→
    63→### 1.2 Interferometric SAR (InSAR)
    64→
    65→InSAR forms an **interferogram** by multiplying one SLC acquisition (reference) with the
    66→complex conjugate of another (secondary):
    67→
    68→```
    69→φ_raw(p) = arg( SLC_ref(p) × conj(SLC_sec(p)) )
    70→```
    71→
    72→The result at each pixel p is a wrapped phase value in (−π, π]. This interferometric
    73→phase contains several contributions:
    74→
    75→```
    76→φ = (4π/λ) × (B_perp / (R × sin(θ))) × Δh   ← topographic term
    77→  + (4π/λ) × Δd                               ← surface deformation term
    78→  + φ_atm                                      ← atmospheric delay term
    79→  + φ_noise                                    ← phase noise (speckle)
    80→```
    81→
    82→where:
    83→- `B_perp` = perpendicular baseline (cross-track separation between two satellite orbits, metres)
    84→- `R` = slant range distance from satellite to target (~500–600 km for Capella)
    85→- `θ` = incidence angle (angle between radar line-of-sight and vertical, typically 30–60°)
    86→- `Δh` = height difference between the topographic surface and the reference ellipsoid
    87→- `Δd` = line-of-sight surface displacement between acquisitions
    88→
    89→**Coherence** γ quantifies how well-correlated the two SLC acquisitions are at each pixel:
    90→
    91→```
    92→γ(p) = |E[SLC_ref × conj(SLC_sec)]| / sqrt(E[|SLC_ref|²] × E[|SLC_sec|²])
    93→```
    94→
    95→γ ∈ [0, 1]: γ=1 means perfect coherence (pure phase signal), γ=0 means complete
    96→decorrelation (pure noise). In practice, γ < 0.35 generally makes phase too noisy
    97→to use for deformation or DEM estimation.
    98→
    99→### 1.3 Phase Noise and the Unwrapping Problem
   100→
   101→The phase noise term φ_noise is the central challenge. It arises from:
   102→- **Temporal decorrelation**: the surface changes between acquisitions (vegetation growth,
   103→  rain, wind, lava flows) — each changed scatterer adds a random phase contribution
   104→- **Geometric decorrelation**: at large baselines B_perp, the same pixel is illuminated
   105→  from a slightly different angle, causing partial decorrelation even for static surfaces
   106→- **Thermal noise**: receiver electronics introduce random phase shifts, dominated by SNR
   107→
   108→The recorded phase is **wrapped** — only the fractional part modulo 2π is observable.
   109→To convert phase to height or displacement, we must first recover the absolute (unwrapped)
   110→phase. This is the **phase unwrapping problem**: integrate the wrapped phase gradient
   111→across the image while handling noise-induced discontinuities.
   112→
   113→Phase unwrapping fails catastrophically where coherence drops below ~0.35. A single
   114→unwrapping error (a 2π jump in the wrong place) propagates along the integration path
   115→and corrupts all downstream measurements in that region. **This is why phase denoising
   116→is so valuable**: a better-denoised interferogram has smoother gradients and fewer
   117→spurious π jumps, leading to more successful unwrapping.
   118→
   119→### 1.4 DEM Generation from InSAR
   120→
   121→Given unwrapped phase φ_unw and known B_perp:
   122→
   123→```
   124→Δh = (φ_unw × λ × R × sin(θ)) / (4π × B_perp)
   125→```
   126→
   127→For pure DEM estimation (no deformation), pairs with short temporal baselines
   128→(Δt < 15 days) minimise the deformation and atmospheric terms.
   129→
   130→**Multi-temporal stacking (SBAS — Small Baseline Subset)** greatly improves accuracy:
   131→a stack of P interferometric pairs can be written as Ax = φ̂ where:
   132→- A is a (P × T) design matrix (+1 at secondary epoch, −1 at reference epoch)
   133→- x is a (T,) vector of epoch-wise phase increments or velocities
   134→- φ̂ is the (P,) vector of observed unwrapped phases
   135→
   136→Solving the weighted least-squares system `min_x ‖W(Ax − φ̂)‖₂` gives a time-consistent
   137→phase model. The residual `‖W(Ax* − φ̂)‖₂` is Contest Metric 5.
   138→
   139→### 1.5 Why Learning-Assisted?
   140→
   141→Classical phase filters (Goldstein-Werner 1998, NL-InSAR 2011, BM3D-SAR 2012) all work
   142→locally and independently per pair. They cannot:
   143→- Adapt to different temporal/geometric baseline regimes in a single model
   144→- Leverage information from the full temporal stack of interferograms
   145→- Propagate uncertainty estimates downstream for weighting
   146→- Directly optimise for the contest evaluation criteria during training
   147→
   148→Deep learning offers all of these — but comes with a fundamental obstacle: **no
   149→ground-truth clean interferograms exist**. Real SAR phase is always contaminated by
   150→noise; there is no way to acquire a "clean" reference for supervised training.
   151→This forces us into **self-supervised** methods that exploit the statistical properties
   152→of the interferometric noise itself.

   222→## 3. Phase 1: Baseline InSAR Pipeline
   223→
   224→All pipeline code lives in `src/insar_processing/` and `scripts/`.
   225→
   226→### 3.1 Pair-Graph Construction (`src/insar_processing/pair_graph.py`)
   227→
   228→Not all combinations of 221 acquisitions make useful interferometric pairs. We use a
   229→quality score Q_ij to rank pairs:
   230→
   231→```
   232→Q_ij = (1 / (Δt + 1)) × (1 / (1 + |Δθ_inc|))
   233→```
   234→
   235→where Δt is the temporal baseline in days and Δθ_inc is the difference in incidence
   236→angle between the two acquisitions. This score balances:
   237→- **Temporal decorrelation**: short Δt → less surface change → higher coherence
   238→- **Geometric diversity**: small |Δθ_inc| → compatible geometry for interferometry
   239→
   240→**Constraints**: both acquisitions must share the same `orbit_state` (ascending/descending)
   241→AND the same `look_direction`. Mixing would put the target on opposite sides of the spacecraft,
   242→making interferometry geometrically nonsensical.
   243→
   244→Results for AOI_000:
   245→- Loose pairs (Δt≤365d, Δinc≤5°): **8,834 pairs** → `data/manifests/hawaii_pairs.parquet`
   246→- Strict pairs (Δt≤60d, Δinc≤2°): **3,033 pairs** → used for closure triplets
   247→- **24,171 closure triplets** → `data/manifests/hawaii_triplets_strict.parquet`
   248→
   249→### 3.2 Perpendicular Baseline Computation (`src/insar_processing/geometry.py`)
   250→
   251→B_perp — the component of the orbital separation vector perpendicular to the
   252→line-of-sight — determines topographic sensitivity and geometric decorrelation.
   253→It is not directly measured; it must be computed from satellite state vectors.
   254→
   255→Algorithm:
   256→1. Parse state vectors from extended JSON (position + velocity at 1-second intervals)
   257→2. Interpolate satellite ECEF (Earth-Centred Earth-Fixed) position at scene centre time
   258→   using cubic spline interpolation for both reference and secondary acquisitions
   259→3. Form the baseline vector: `B = pos_sec − pos_ref` (in ECEF coordinates)
   260→4. Compute the look vector `l̂` (unit vector from satellite to scene centre in ECEF)
   261→5. Decompose B into parallel (along l̂) and perpendicular components:
   262→   `B_perp = |B − (B · l̂) × l̂|`
   263→
   264→Hawaii statistics: mean B_perp = 35 m, median = 6 m, max = 40 km.
   265→The wide range (6 m to 40 km) is important: larger B_perp pairs have higher topographic
   266→sensitivity but more geometric decorrelation — this diversity is critical for FiLM training.
   267→
   268→### 3.3 Coregistration (`scripts/preprocess_pairs.py`)
   269→
   270→SLC acquisitions from different orbits are not naturally aligned — each orbit places
   271→pixels at slightly different positions on the ground. Before forming an interferogram,
   272→the secondary SLC must be registered to the reference SLC at sub-pixel accuracy.
   273→
   274→Algorithm (phase cross-correlation):
   275→1. Compute 2D FFT of the amplitude (|SLC|) images of reference and secondary
   276→2. Form the normalised cross-power spectrum:
   277→   `R = FFT(|ref|) × conj(FFT(|sec|)) / |R|`
   278→   (phase-only normalisation: unit amplitude, only phase carries shift information)
   279→3. IFFT(R) → correlation peak location (integer pixel offset)
   280→4. Sub-pixel refinement via DFT upsampling (Guizar-Sicairos 2008):
   281→   compute a local DFT around the integer peak at 10× upsampling to achieve
   282→   1/10-pixel registration accuracy
   283→5. Apply shift: linear phase ramp in frequency domain
   284→   `SLC_sec_shifted = IFFT( FFT(SLC_sec) × exp(−j2π × (Δr×f_r + Δa×f_a)) )`
   285→   This is the exact, interpolation-free shift for band-limited signals
   286→
   287→### 3.4 Interferogram Formation
   288→
   289→```
   290→ifg(p) = SLC_ref(p) × conj(SLC_sec_coreg(p))
   291→```
   292→
   293→The raw interferogram is a complex image where:
   294→- Phase: `∠ifg(p) = φ_topo + φ_defo + φ_atm + φ_noise` (all contributions summed)
   295→- Amplitude: `|ifg(p)|` = product of backscatter amplitudes (not used directly)
   296→
   297→For network input we normalise to unit amplitude (extract only phase):
   298→```
   299→ifg_norm(p) = ifg(p) / (|ifg(p)| + ε)
   300→```
   301→This gives a complex image with |ifg_norm| ≈ 1 everywhere; Re and Im form the two input
   302→channels to the model.
   303→
   304→### 3.5 Coherence Estimation
   305→
   306→Using a spatial (box-car) estimator with a 5×5 multi-look window:
   307→
   308→```
   309→γ(p) = |mean_{window}( SLC_ref × conj(SLC_sec) )| / sqrt( mean(|SLC_ref|²) × mean(|SLC_sec|²) )
   310→```
   311→
   312→Implemented via `scipy.ndimage.uniform_filter` for efficiency on large arrays.
   313→The coherence map serves three roles:
   314→1. Quality indicator for pair selection and usability gating (γ > 0.35)
   315→2. FiLM conditioning input (SNR proxy)
   316→3. SNAPHU pixel weighting (low-coherence pixels cost more to unwrap)
   317→
   318→### 3.6 Goldstein-Werner Spectral Filter (`src/insar_processing/filters.py`)
   319→
   320→The Goldstein-Werner filter (Goldstein & Werner 1998) is the standard classical baseline:
   321→
   322→1. Partition the interferogram into overlapping 32×32 blocks (8-pixel overlap)
   323→2. For each block: compute 2D FFT
   324→3. Smooth the spectral magnitude: `|S_smooth| = uniform_filter(|S|, size=3)`
   325→4. Power-law spectral weighting: `W_block = (|S_smooth| / max(|S_smooth|))^α`
   326→5. Apply: `S_filtered = S × W_block` then IFFT
   327→6. Overlap-add with Hanning taper to reconstruct the filtered image
   328→
   329→The parameter α ∈ [0,1] controls filter strength: α=0 is no filtering; α=1 is maximum.
   330→
   331→Our coherence-adaptive variant:
   332→```
   333→α(block) = α_min + (α_max − α_min) × (1 − γ_block)
   334→```
   335→Low-coherence blocks (γ→0) receive maximum filtering (α→α_max);
   336→high-coherence blocks (γ→1) receive minimum filtering (α→α_min).
   337→This preserves fine fringe detail where the signal is trustworthy.
   338→
   339→### 3.7 Sub-look Splitting for Noise2Noise (`src/insar_processing/sublook.py`)
   340→
   341→Self-supervised training via Noise2Noise requires **two independent noisy observations
   342→of the same underlying scene**. We generate these via frequency sub-banding:
   343→
   344→- **Method 1 (FFT azimuth sub-banding)**: split the SLC spectrum into lower and upper
   345→  frequency halves in azimuth. Each half forms its own sub-look interferogram.
   346→  Because the two frequency bands are orthogonal, their speckle realisations are
   347→  statistically independent: `Cov(φ_sub_A, φ_sub_B) ≈ 0`
   348→  Verified: sub-look phase correlation = 0.001 (effectively zero) ✓
   349→  Advantage: preserves full range resolution; no aliasing artefacts
   350→
   351→- **Method 2 (odd/even lines)**: alternating azimuth lines to each sub-look.
   352→  Simpler but halves azimuth resolution.
   353→
   354→The resulting pair (ifg_A, ifg_B) has:
   355→- `E[φ_sub_A] = E[φ_sub_B] = φ_true` (same mean = true interferometric phase)
   356→- Independent noise (speckle decorrelated across the two bands)
   357→
   358→This satisfies the Noise2Noise condition exactly: training f(φ_A) to predict φ_B
   359→converges to the MMSE estimator `E[φ_true | φ_A]`.
   360→
   361→### 3.8 Outputs Per Processed Pair
   362→
   363→For each of the 100 preprocessed pairs, `scripts/preprocess_pairs.py` writes to
   364→`data/processed/pairs/{pair_id}/`:
   365→
   366→| File | Description |
   367→|------|-------------|

   375→## 4. Phase 2: FiLM-Conditioned U-Net (FiLMUNet)
   376→
   377→### 4.1 Motivation: Why Geometry-Conditioned Denoising?
   378→
   379→Phase noise in InSAR is not stationary — it depends strongly on acquisition geometry:
   380→- Longer temporal baseline Δt → more decorrelation → stronger noise
   381→- Larger B_perp → more geometric decorrelation → fringe-dependent noise
   382→- Different incidence angles → different scattering geometry → different coherence patterns
   383→
   384→A model trained on a mixture of short-Δt (coherent) and long-Δt (noisy) pairs without
   385→geometry awareness would learn a weighted average of these noise regimes, performing
   386→suboptimally for both. FiLM conditioning allows the **same model** to adapt its
   387→behaviour to the current pair's geometry at inference time.
   388→
   389→Additionally, FiLM conditioning enables **zero-shot transfer**: a model trained on
   390→Hawaii can be applied to LA (AOI_008) with different incidence angles and baselines
   391→without retraining — the geometry conditioning vector handles the domain shift.
   392→
   393→### 4.2 Feature-wise Linear Modulation (FiLM) Primer
   394→
   395→FiLM (Perez et al. 2018) conditions a neural network on external information by applying
   396→learned affine transformations to intermediate feature maps:
   397→
   398→```
   399→FiLM(x; c) = γ(c) ⊙ x + β(c)
   400→```
   401→
   402→where x ∈ R^C is a feature map, c is the conditioning vector, and γ(c), β(c) ∈ R^C
   403→are channel-wise scale and shift predicted by a small MLP. Applied after batch
   404→normalisation (before activation), this is equivalent to replacing the fixed BN affine
   405→parameters with input-dependent ones.
   406→
   407→Our residual-centred variant uses `y = (1 + γ(c)) ⊙ x + β(c)`, so at initialisation
   408→(γ→0, β→0) the modulation is identity — this stabilises early training.
   409→
   410→### 4.3 Architecture Overview
   411→
   412→```
   413→Input:
   414→  x     : (B, 3, H, W)   — Re(ifg), Im(ifg), coherence
   415→  meta  : (B, 7)          — [Δt, θ_inc, θ_graze, B_perp, mode, look, SNR_proxy]
   416→                            (standardised by known physical ranges)
   417→
   418→MetadataEncoder:
   419→  Linear(7 → 64) → ReLU → Linear(64 → 64) → ReLU   →  embed: (B, 64)
   420→
   421→Encoder (each level: FiLMDoubleConv + MaxPool(2)):
   422→  Level 0: (B,  3, H,   W  ) → (B,  32, H/2,  W/2 )
   423→  Level 1: (B, 32, H/2, W/2) → (B,  64, H/4,  W/4 )
   424→  Level 2: (B, 64, H/4, W/4) → (B, 128, H/8,  W/8 )
   425→  Level 3: (B,128, H/8, W/8) → (B, 256, H/16, W/16)
   426→
   427→Bottleneck:
   428→  FiLMDoubleConv(256 → 512)
   429→
   430→Decoder (each level: ConvTranspose(2×) + skip-cat + FiLMDoubleConv):
   431→  Level 3: (512+256 → 256)
   432→  Level 2: (256+128 → 128)
   433→  Level 1: (128+ 64 →  64)
   434→  Level 0: ( 64+ 32 →  32)
   435→
   436→Output heads (1×1 convolutions):
   437→  head_denoised : Conv(32 → 2)  → denoised Re+Im interferogram
   438→  head_log_var  : Conv(32 → 1)  → per-pixel log(σ²) uncertainty
   439→```
   440→
   441→### 4.4 FiLMDoubleConv Block Detail
   442→
   443→Each encoder/decoder block is a double convolution with FiLM modulation injected
   444→after the first batch normalisation:
   445→
   446→```
   447→x → Conv(3×3, pad=1, no bias) → BatchNorm → FiLM(embed) → ReLU
   448→  → Conv(3×3, pad=1, no bias) → BatchNorm → ReLU
   449→```
   450→
   451→FiLM after the first BN is the key design choice: it allows the metadata embedding to
   452→shift and scale the normalised activations before the non-linearity, maximising the
   453→conditioning signal's influence on the feature representation.
   454→

   478→### 4.7 Per-Pixel Uncertainty Output
   479→
   480→The `head_log_var` output enables downstream uncertainty-weighted processing.
   481→Interpreting the output as aleatoric (data-driven) uncertainty:
   482→
   483→```
   484→σ²(p) = exp(log_var(p))    ← predicted variance at pixel p
   485→```
   486→
   487→This uncertainty has three downstream uses:
   488→1. **SNAPHU weighting**: replace uniform pixel weights with `1/σ²(p)` — less confident
   489→   pixels cost more to cross during network-flow unwrapping
   490→2. **SBAS inversion**: `W_p = 1/σ²(p)` in weighted least-squares — temporally inconsistent
   491→   pixels (high uncertainty) have lower weight in the epoch-velocity solution
   492→3. **Closure-weighted loss**: `w(p) = 1/σ²(p)` in L_closure during training

   496→## 5. Phase 3: Self-Supervised Physics Loss Suite
   497→
   498→### 5.1 Why Self-Supervised?
   499→
   500→A fundamental obstacle in InSAR deep learning is that **no ground-truth clean
   501→interferograms exist**. All real SAR data is contaminated by speckle noise; the
   502→"clean" underlying phase is a theoretical construct, not an observable quantity.
   503→
   504→This rules out supervised training (no (noisy, clean) pairs) and most forms of
   505→semi-supervised learning. We instead exploit two intrinsic properties of interferometric
   506→phase to define self-supervised objectives:
   507→1. **Statistical independence of sub-look speckle** → Noise2Noise training
   508→2. **Topological closure of triplet phase cycles** → physics-consistency training
   509→
   510→Combined, these five loss components directly optimise for the contest evaluation criteria
   511→without requiring any external reference data.
   512→
   513→### 5.2 Loss 1 — Noise2Noise (L_n2n, weight=1.0)
   514→
   515→**Principle** (Lehtinen et al. 2018): Given two independent noisy observations y₁, y₂
   516→of the same underlying signal x where E[y₁] = E[y₂] = x, training f(y₁) to minimise
   517→‖f(y₁) − y₂‖² converges to the MMSE estimator f*(y) = E[x|y]. This holds for any
   518→zero-mean-noise distribution, not just Gaussian.
   519→
   520→**InSAR application**: Sub-look A and sub-look B have the same expected wrapped phase
   521→(= true interferometric phase), with independent speckle noise. Therefore:
   522→```
   523→L_n2n = mean( |wrap( φ_pred − φ_sublook_B )| )
   524→```
   525→
   526→We use wrapped-phase L1 rather than amplitude L1 (Re/Im residual) because:
   527→- Phase is the physically meaningful quantity for InSAR (not amplitude)
   528→- Wrapping discontinuities cause large amplitude residuals at ±π boundaries that do not
   529→  correspond to actual prediction errors
   530→- Phase-domain L1 is robust to these wrap artefacts
   531→
   532→**Current implementation**: in the absence of actual sub-look pairs in the tile data,
   533→the batch is split in half and each half serves as the other's "target". This is a
   534→simplified proxy; the production version uses actual FFT sub-band pairs.
   535→
   536→### 5.3 Loss 2 — Uncertainty NLL (L_unc, weight=0.5)
   537→
   538→Heteroscedastic negative log-likelihood forces the uncertainty prediction to be
   539→calibrated (neither overconfident nor trivially infinite):
   540→
   541→```
   542→L_unc = mean( 0.5 × exp(−log_σ²) × |wrap(φ_pred − φ_target)|² + 0.5 × log_σ² )
   543→```
   544→
   545→The two terms create competing pressures:
   546→- **Accuracy term** `exp(−log_σ²) × err²`: large residuals penalised more when predicted
   547→  σ² is small — forces the model to be accurate where it claims confidence
   548→- **Regularisation term** `log_σ²`: penalises large σ² — prevents trivial solution
   549→  where model predicts infinite uncertainty everywhere to make accuracy term vanish
   550→
   551→This is the NLL of an isotropic Laplace distribution:
   552→`log p(φ | μ, σ²) ∝ −|φ − μ|/σ − log(2σ)`, reparametrised with σ² = exp(log_σ²).
   553→
   554→### 5.4 Loss 3 — Triplet Closure Consistency (L_closure, weight=0.3)
   555→
   556→**Physical principle**: for three SAR acquisitions i, j, k with well-defined geometry,
   557→the interferometric phases must satisfy the closure condition:
   558→```
   559→wrap(φ_ij + φ_jk − φ_ik) = 0
   560→```
   561→Any non-zero closure is pure noise (phase noise is not path-independent, but the
   562→underlying signal is). Minimising closure error during training directly optimises
   563→Contest Metric 1.
   564→
   565→**Implementation**:
   566→```
   567→L_closure = mean( w(p) × [1 − cos( wrap(φ̂_ij + φ̂_jk − φ̂_ik) )] )
   568→```
   569→
   570→where w(p) = coherence or 1/σ²(p) weighting. The cosine loss is bounded [0, 2]:
   571→- Perfect closure: cos(0) = 1 → loss = 0
   572→- Maximum error: cos(±π) = −1 → loss = 2
   573→- Differentiable everywhere except at cos(±π) (measure-zero set)
   574→
   575→### 5.5 Loss 4 — Temporal Consistency (L_temporal, weight=0.2)
   576→
   577→The SBAS model `Ax = φ̂` provides a multi-temporal consistency constraint. We embed
   578→this as a differentiable loss:
   579→
   580→```
   581→x* = weighted_lstsq(A, φ̂; W=diag(w))       ← closed-form via torch.linalg.lstsq
   582→L_temporal = ‖W^{1/2} × (A×x* − φ̂)‖₂²    ← weighted SBAS residual
   583→```
   584→
   585→By minimising this loss during training, the network learns to produce denoised phases
   586→that are inherently consistent with the multi-temporal phase model — the SBAS inversion
   587→residual is minimised **before** the inversion is even run.
   588→
   589→This is a novel approach: SBAS is traditionally a post-processing step after denoising.
   590→Embedding it as a training loss creates a direct gradient pathway to Contest Metric 5.
   591→
   592→### 5.6 Loss 5 — Gradient Preservation (L_grad, weight=0.1)
   593→
   594→Prevents over-smoothing and preserves interferometric fringe structure:
   595→
   596→```
   597→L_grad = mean( |∇_y wrap(φ_pred) − ∇_y wrap(φ_input)|
   598→             + |∇_x wrap(φ_pred) − ∇_x wrap(φ_input)| ) / 2
   599→```
   600→
   601→Operates on **wrapped phase gradients** (not on Re/Im amplitude gradients) to correctly
   602→handle fringe discontinuities. The low weight (0.1) makes this a structural regulariser
   603→rather than a primary supervision signal.
   604→
   605→### 5.7 Combined Loss
   606→
   607→```
   608→L_total = 1.0 × L_n2n
   609→        + 0.5 × L_unc
   610→        + 0.3 × L_closure
   611→        + 0.2 × L_temporal
   612→        + 0.1 × L_grad
   613→```
   614→
   615→Weight rationale:
   616→- N2N (1.0): dominant self-supervised signal — drives the core denoising
   617→- Uncertainty (0.5): important secondary objective for downstream calibration
   618→- Closure (0.3): geometry-consistency regulariser, directly tied to Metric 1
   619→- Temporal (0.2): stack consistency, directly tied to Metric 5
   620→- Gradient (0.1): lightweight structural prior, prevents fringe destruction
   621→

   665→## 7. Evaluation Metrics (5 Contest KPIs)
   666→
   667→All five metrics are implemented in `src/evaluation/closure_metrics.py`.
   668→
   669→### 7.1 Metric 1 — Triplet Closure Error
   670→
   671→```
   672→closure_error(i,j,k) = wrap( φ_ij + φ_jk − φ_ik )
   673→Report: median|closure|, mean|closure|, std(closure), RMSE(closure)
   674→Target: median|closure| ↓ ≥30% vs Goldstein-only baseline
   675→```
   676→
   677→Physical meaning: perfectly coherent phase has zero closure. Non-zero closure indicates
   678→phase noise or non-linear atmospheric artefacts. Lower closure = better denoising.
   679→
   680→Evaluated over all 24,171 strict triplets from the Hawaii pair-graph manifest.
   681→
   682→**Model linkage**: L_closure during training directly minimises this metric.
   683→
   684→### 7.2 Metric 2 — Unwrap Success Rate
   685→
   686→```
   687→success_rate = |{p : γ(p) ≥ 0.35 AND unw_phase(p) ≠ NaN}|
   688→             / |{p : γ(p) ≥ 0.35}|
   689→Target: ↑ ≥15 percentage points vs Goldstein-only baseline
   690→```
   691→
   692→Only coherent pixels (γ ≥ 0.35) are evaluated — asking for unwrapping success in
   693→incoherent regions is not meaningful. A pixel is "successfully unwrapped" if SNAPHU
   694→assigns it a finite phase value (not NaN / masked out).
   695→
   696→**Model linkage**: FiLMUNet denoising smooths phase gradients → fewer SNAPHU failures
   697→at phase transition regions. Uncertainty output `σ²(p)` used as SNAPHU pixel weight.
   698→
   699→### 7.3 Metric 3 — Percent Usable Pairs
   700→
   701→```
   702→usable(pair) = (mean_γ ≥ 0.35) AND (median|closure| < 0.5 rad)
   703→usable_fraction = |{usable pairs}| / |{all pairs}|
   704→Target: ↑ ≥25 percentage points vs Goldstein-only baseline
   705→```
   706→
   707→A dual gate: a pair must be both coherent (passes decorrelation test) and geometrically
   708→consistent (passes closure test). This is the most stringent metric — it catches pairs
   709→that look coherent but have systematic biases.
   710→
   711→**Model linkage**: FiLMUNet improves both coherence (indirectly, via denoising) and
   712→closure (directly, via L_closure). Both gates relax → fraction increases.
   713→
   714→### 7.4 Metric 4 — DEM NMAD
   715→
   716→NMAD (Normalised Median Absolute Deviation) is a robust estimator of DEM height error:
   717→
   718→```
   719→e(p) = h_estimated(p) − h_reference(p)        ← per-pixel height error
   720→NMAD = 1.4826 × median( |e − median(e)| )
   721→Target: ↓ ≥15% vs Goldstein-only baseline
   722→```
   723→
   724→The factor 1.4826 = 1 / Φ⁻¹(0.75) normalises MAD to be consistent with σ for a
   725→Gaussian distribution. NMAD is resistant to outliers (unlike RMSE) and preferred for
   726→DEM accuracy assessment.
   727→
   728→Evaluated on stable, coherent terrain pixels with low slope (to avoid layover/shadow).
   729→Requires a reference DEM (external, not part of the contest dataset — use NASADEM or
   730→Copernicus DEM 30m as reference).
   731→
   732→**Model linkage**: Improved phase denoising → better unwrapping → better SBAS inversion
   733→→ lower height errors. Uncertainty-weighted SBAS (W = 1/σ²) is the primary mechanism.
   734→
   735→### 7.5 Metric 5 — Temporal Consistency Residual
   736→
   737→```
   738→x* = argmin_x ‖W^{1/2}(Ax − φ̂)‖₂²     ← weighted SBAS inversion
   739→residual = ‖W^{1/2}(Ax* − φ̂)‖₂
   740→W = diag(1/σ²(p))                        ← FiLMUNet uncertainty as weights
   741→Target: ↓ ≥20% vs uniform-weight SBAS baseline
   742→```
   743→
   744→Design matrix A has shape (P × T) where P = number of pairs, T = number of epochs.
   745→Each row has +1 at the secondary epoch index and −1 at the reference epoch index.
   746→
   747→**Model linkage**: L_temporal during training directly minimises this residual.
   748→FiLMUNet's `σ²(p)` output replaces uniform weights in W, further reducing the residual
   749→by down-weighting temporally inconsistent pixels.

   753→## 8. SNAPHU Phase Unwrapping
   754→
   755→### 8.1 SNAPHU Overview
   756→
   757→SNAPHU (Statistical-cost Network-Flow Phase Unwrapping, Chen & Zebker 2001) solves
   758→the phase unwrapping problem by minimising a cost function defined on a network graph
   759→where each pixel is a node and adjacent-pixel phase differences are edges.
   760→
   761→Two operating modes:
   762→- **TOPO** (topographic): L2 cost norm, appropriate for DEM estimation
   763→- **DEFO** (deformation): Bayesian cost, appropriate for subsidence/uplift
   764→
   765→For DEM estimation (our use case) we use TOPO mode. Coherence maps provide per-pixel
   766→weighting: low-coherence pixels have high edge cost and the network-flow solver
   767→preferentially routes the unwrapping path away from them.
   768→
   769→### 8.2 Implementation (`scripts/unwrap_snaphu.py`)
   770→
   771→The script wraps the SNAPHU command-line binary with automated configuration:
   772→
   773→1. **Read inputs**: `ifg_goldstein.tif` (or FiLMUNet denoised output) → wrapped phase
   774→   via `arctan2(Im_band, Re_band)`; `coherence.tif` for weighting
   775→2. **Masking**: pixels with γ < 0.1 are set to NaN — too incoherent to unwrap reliably
   776→3. **Write binary inputs**: phase and coherence as flat float32 arrays (SNAPHU format)
   777→4. **Write config file**: `LINELENGTH`, `NLINES`, `NCORRLOOKS=9`, `INITMETHOD=MST`
   778→5. **Auto-tiling** for large images:
   779→   - > 8192 px: 4×4 tile decomposition
   780→   - > 4096 px: 2×2 tile decomposition
   781→   - Smaller: no tiling
   782→6. **Run SNAPHU** as subprocess; capture exit code
   783→7. **Read output**: parse unwrapped phase binary → `unw_phase.tif` (float32 GeoTIFF,
   784→   NaN at masked pixels, original CRS/transform preserved)
   785→
   786→Additional features:
   787→- Skip already-processed pairs (`unw_phase.tif` exists)
   788→- Graceful exit with warning if SNAPHU binary not on PATH
   789→- `--help` flag confirmed working
   790→

   792→

   794→- SNAPHU binary: **not yet installed** (blocked on: `conda install -c conda-forge snaphu`)
   795→- Will be run on all 100 processed pairs after SNAPHU installation

   800→## 9. Novelty and Scientific Contribution
   801→
   802→### 9.1 Prior Art Review: InSAR Phase Denoising
   803→
   804→**Classical approaches:**
   805→- **Goldstein-Werner (1998)**: power-law spectral weighting on overlapping 32×32 blocks.
   806→  Widely used, fast. Weaknesses: purely local (no cross-block context), fixed scalar α
   807→  regardless of geometry or temporal baseline, no uncertainty output.
   808→- **NL-InSAR (Deledalle et al. 2011)**: non-local means adapted to InSAR complex domain.
   809→  Stronger than Goldstein for homogeneous scenes. Weaknesses: computationally expensive
   810→  (hours per image), no temporal awareness, no geometry conditioning.
   811→- **BM3D-SAR (Parrilli 2012)**: block-matching 3D collaborative filtering.
   812→  Best classical baseline, extremely slow, non-geometry-aware, no uncertainty output.
   813→
   814→**Supervised DL approaches (2018–2024):**
   815→- **DeepInSAR (Anantrasirichai et al. 2021)**: ResNet for InSAR phase, supervised on
   816→  synthetic interferograms from DEM + deformation model. Weaknesses: large domain gap
   817→  (synthetic vs. real X-band); fixed geometry; no uncertainty output.
   818→- **InSAR-DNN / PhaseNet variants**: U-Nets applied to wrapped phase tiles. Weaknesses:
   819→  require reference clean data (not available for Capella); no temporal or geometric
   820→  awareness; each pair processed independently.
   821→- **RNN/ConvLSTM for time-series InSAR**: temporal awareness but still supervised; no
   822→  explicit closure enforcement.
   823→
   824→**Self-supervised SAR work (2022–2024):**
   825→- **MERLIN (Dalsasso et al. 2021)**: self-supervised SAR despeckling via sub-look pairs
   826→  for intensity (amplitude). No phase/coherence handling, no geometry conditioning,
   827→  no InSAR-specific losses.
   828→- **SAR-Noise2Noise (Molini et al. 2022)**: Noise2Noise on SAR intensity. Correct
   829→  framework, wrong modality — intensity N2N does not handle interferometric phase.
   830→
   831→**Key gap in all prior work**: no prior method combines (a) self-supervised training on
   832→real complex InSAR data, (b) geometry conditioning for cross-baseline generalisation,
   833→(c) per-pixel uncertainty for downstream use, and (d) physics-consistency losses that
   834→directly optimise the evaluation criteria.
   835→

   837→
   838→**Contribution 1 — Geometry-Conditioned Self-Supervised InSAR Denoiser**
   839→

   841→SNR_proxy] via FiLM modulation applied at every encoder/decoder block. A single model

   864→interpretation and does not directly optimise closure.
   865→
   866→Contest link: direct gradient flow to Metric 1; indirect improvement to Metric 2.

   869→
   870→`head_log_var` predicts per-pixel log(σ²) via heteroscedastic NLL. This uncertainty
   871→weights both SNAPHU and SBAS — replacing coherence (an input proxy) with a learned,
   872→geometry-aware confidence estimate.
   873→
   874→No prior InSAR DL method outputs uncertainty; no prior method uses learned uncertainty
   875→as the SBAS weight matrix.
   876→
   877→Contest link: uncertainty-weighted SBAS directly targets Metric 5; uncertainty-weighted
   878→SNAPHU targets Metrics 2, 3.
   879→
   880→**Contribution 5 — SBAS Temporal Consistency as Training Loss**
   881→
   882→L_temporal embeds a differentiable SBAS residual as a training loss, teaching the model
   883→to produce phase stacks that satisfy the temporal model before post-processing.
   884→
   885→Embedding SBAS as a training loss is novel. The gradient flows from temporal consistency
   886→back through all pairs in the batch simultaneously.
   887→
   888→Contest link: direct gradient flow to Metric 5.
